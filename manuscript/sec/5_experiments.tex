\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We attach the same update layer to three mappers and test whether obsolete states leave the map, whether the coverage
of earlier runs remains, and whether the new run still maps its own changes. We also run each mapper in the ways it
handles a revisit without our layer.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Datasets.}
\emph{Real room.} We record one room three times with a Kinect: run A (133\,s, 4{,}003 frames), run B (135\,s,
4{,}041 frames) about six hours later, and run C (241\,s, 7{,}216 frames) twelve days after B. B starts from A's
exported state and C from B's, and rigid transforms align B and C with A. The room holds 20 objects. Between A and B,
seven moved objects, one addition, and one removal give 16 appearance or disappearance events. Between B and C, four
moved objects and two removals give 10 events. One cabinet changes out of view once in A, twice in B, and twice in C.
Object reference surfaces come from RGB-D measurements aggregated over annotated presence intervals.

% SRC event counts: manuscript (GitHub main, 2026-09-28), Sec. 5.1
\emph{Simulated apartment.} Two Isaac Sim runs observe one furnished apartment: A with 4{,}186 frames and B with
3{,}555 frames at 30\,Hz, with exact poses, semantics, and instance labels. B starts from A's final state and
observes only part of what A mapped. The complete scene mesh is the reference inside and outside the current camera coverage.

\paragraph{Compared methods.}
We compare against published methods on the same recordings: Khronos~\cite{schmid2024khronos}, which maps each run
with dynamic and change reasoning but starts every run from an empty map; Panoptic Multi-TSDFs~\cite{schmid2022panoptic},
which keeps object submaps that become persistent, absent, or unobserved across visits; GaME~\cite{yugay2026game},
which updates a Gaussian map after changes out of view; and wavemap~\cite{reijgwart2023wavemap}, whose log-odds update
clears the cells that rays traverse. We run our layer on three backends: a Khronos TSDF pipeline, GaME, and wavemap.
Each backend keeps its code, inputs, and resolution, so the difference to its published counterpart is our layer.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
\emph{Online object F1} evaluates the map of every moment against the objects present at that moment, weighted by the
duration of each state~\cite{schmid2024khronos}; it scores the current map and its history together.
\emph{Change F1} counts appearance and disappearance events. For reference surface samples $G$ and a predicted map $M$,
$r(G,M)$ is the fraction of samples within 10\,cm of predicted surface of the same class, and a state is represented
when $r \ge 0.5$. A moved object gives two events, its old state disappearing and its new state appearing. We report
changes within a run, which happen in view or out of view, and changes between runs.
\emph{Old-site residue} is the fraction of old object surface that remains where later measurements observe free space.
Lower is better.
\emph{Geometry} is the F1 at 5\,cm between surface samples of the map and the reference. The simulated reference is the
complete scene mesh, including surfaces that the last run leaves unobserved. The real reference is reconstructed from
each run's own RGB-D data and covers what that run observes. We score each backend on its own surface: the TSDF mesh,
the occupied cells, and a TSDF fused from depth rendered from the Gaussians~\cite{huang20242dgs}.
\emph{Rendering} of the Gaussian maps follows GaME's protocol~\cite{yugay2026game}: PSNR, MS-SSIM~\cite{wang2003msssim},
LPIPS~\cite{zhang2018lpips}, and depth L1 on every tenth frame of the last run, held out from mapping. \emph{Own time}
renders each frame with the map of its own timestamp, $\mathcal{M}(t_k)$.
A cell reads n/a when the method lacks the capability: Khronos relates no two runs, Panoptic Multi-TSDFs and wavemap
keep no timeline of their map, and only Gaussian maps render. With our layer, every backend keeps element lifetimes
and therefore a timeline.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
Simulated runs use ground-truth poses, and real runs use the rigid alignment above. All datasets and backends share
the constants, and evaluation ground truth played no part in choosing them. Each configuration runs once.

\Cref{tab:main_real,tab:main_sim} compare each backend with and without our layer against its published counterpart.
The online object F1 scores the map of every moment, its history included. With the TSDF backend, it rises from 91.5
and 83.8 for Khronos to 99.2 and 98.3 in the real runs B and C, and from 66.8 to 91.3 in the simulated run B. Khronos
starts each run from an empty map, so it relates no two runs; our layer carries the object states across them. With
the Gaussian backend, the online object F1 stays within 1.4 points of GaME in every run. Wavemap keeps no timeline;
with our layer, the occupancy map gains one and reaches online object F1 89.5 and 95.4 on the simulated apartment.

Our layer also gets the state changes right. Within a run, the TSDF backend detects every change on the real room and
reaches F1 100 and 96.3 on the simulated apartment, against 66.7 in the real runs B and C and 67.9 and 88.0 in the
simulated runs for Khronos. Between runs, all three backends detect 15
of 16 changes from A to B and all 10 from B to C on the real room, and 29 of 32 on the simulated apartment, against
64.0, 37.5, and 91.8 for Panoptic Multi-TSDFs, 93.3, 94.7, and 81.5 for GaME, and 93.3, 100, and 87.7 for wavemap.

Obsolete surfaces leave the map. With our layer, 1.6\% to 11.5\% of the old object surface remains at observed-empty
sites, against 57.1\% and 60.1\% for Panoptic Multi-TSDFs and 42.4\% and 61.1\% for GaME. Wavemap removes old surfaces
too (0.6\% and 0.0\%), but its rays also clear surfaces that remain: its real geometry F1 falls to 83.9 and 85.0 in B
and C, against 89.7 and 89.5 with our layer. On the real room, the TSDF backend reaches geometry F1 98.6, 97.3, and 98.4
in runs A, B, and C, against 91.0, 95.9, and 94.7 for Khronos. On the complete simulated reference, our layer reaches geometry F1 98.5
(TSDF), 96.6 (Gaussians), and 93.4 (occupancy), against 83.6 to 92.1 for the published methods.

The Gaussian map keeps its rendering quality. On held-out frames of the last run, it renders 0.81\,dB (simulated) and
0.73\,dB (real) better than GaME. Rendering each frame with the map of its own time raises PSNR by another 1.03\,dB
and 0.24\,dB, because objects also change during the last run. GaME keeps only its final map and cannot render an earlier
moment.

Each backend shows where its own update fails (\cref{tab:ablation_variants}). A scratch map recalls 16.4 to 24.3 points less of
the simulated scene than our layer on the same backend, because it lacks the surfaces that only earlier runs
observed. Carrying an occupancy map over without change handling keeps
the recall but also 96.4\% and 97.3\% of the old surfaces. GaME's update and wavemap's update each fix one side:
GaME keeps recall and leaves residue, and wavemap removes residue and loses recall. On the occupancy backend, our layer keeps
the recall of the carried map (88.1\%) and removes most of its residue (6.2\% and 2.6\%). On the Gaussian backend, it
raises both precision and recall over GaME's update.
