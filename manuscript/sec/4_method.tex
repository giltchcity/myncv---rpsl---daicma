\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

Our method keeps a map correct while objects in the scene move, appear, and disappear, during a visit and between
visits. It takes posed RGB-D frames with SAM masks~\cite{kirillov2023sam}, which give every pixel an object identity
that persists across frames, and, on a revisit, the map and the object states saved by the previous visit.
\Cref{fig:method} shows the two steps that every depth image goes through. Change detection compares the image with the
surfaces of the current object states and turns the comparison into state intervals: a state ends when its surface is
gone, continues while the camera keeps finding it, and begins when an object appears (\cref{sec:states}). Map update
applies these intervals to the map, so that the geometry of an ended state leaves the map and every observation updates
only the geometry that held when it was taken (\cref{sec:validity}). Both steps operate on object states and capture
times rather than on voxels, Gaussians, or cells, which is why the same mechanism runs on a TSDF, a 3D Gaussian map, and
an occupancy map (\cref{sec:representations}).

\subsection{Object States and Map Elements}
\label{sec:setting}

We describe the scene by object states. Each object $\ell$ passes through a sequence of static states, and state $s$ has
a surface $G_s$, reconstructed from the observations of $\ell$ while it stayed in that state, and an interval
$[b_s, d_s)$ in which it holds. When the armchair of \cref{fig:method} is carried away, its state ends. If it is put
down elsewhere, a new state begins there and is built from the observations of its new place alone. An observation $o$
is the depth reading of one pixel, with range $r_o$, capture time $t_o$, and instance label $\ell_o$. The map consists of
elements $\mathcal{E}$: the surface faces of a TSDF, the Gaussians of a Gaussian map, or the occupied cells of an
occupancy map. Each element $e$ has a position $x_e$, a half-size $h_e$ (half a voxel, three standard deviations of a
Gaussian, or half a cell), an instance label $\ell_e$ that is $0$ for the background, and a lifetime $[c_e, d_e)$, and
the elements of a state end with it. The map at time $t$ is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{M}(t) = \{\, e \in \mathcal{E} : c_e \le t < d_e \,\}.
  \label{eq:map_at_t}
\end{equation}
Ending an element closes its lifetime rather than deleting it, so the map can still be queried at any earlier time.

\subsection{Change Detection with State Intervals}
\label{sec:states}

Detecting a change is hard because the camera sees only part of the scene. A surface that the camera does not look at
has not disappeared, and one frame that sees through a small patch does not show that the whole armchair is gone. A
detector that decides frame by frame therefore removes surfaces that noise or occlusion only seem to clear, while one
that waits until it has seen a whole object again reacts late. We test the surfaces of the map directly against the
current depth instead, and we end a state as soon as the readings have covered enough of its surface.

\paragraph{Testing surfaces with the current depth.}
Every later depth image tests the current states. We project the reliable surface samples of $G_s$ into the image,
leaving out thin parts, edges, and mislabeled pixels, whose readings pass the surface even when it is in place. A sample
is \emph{supported} when the reading at its pixel lies within a sensor tolerance of its depth, \emph{seen through} when
the reading lies farther, and \emph{occluded} when it lies closer. Only supported and seen-through samples count as
evidence. An occluded sample, a sample outside the image, or a pixel without depth says nothing about the surface, so
occlusion never ends a state.

\paragraph{Accumulating evidence over the surface.}
A surface in place is still seen through at a small fraction of its samples, because depth is noisy and frames are not
perfectly aligned. For depth image $i$, which judges $n_i$ samples of $G_s$ and sees through $k_i$ of them, we model the
fraction $f_i = k_i/n_i$ of a state in place by a Beta density $p_s$. This density is learned from the frames in which
the object stayed in place, and it is drawn toward the statistics of all objects while the object has few such frames.
Under a change, $f$ is uniform. A Page statistic~\cite{page1954cusum} accumulates the evidence from the unknown first
image $j$ after the change:
\begin{equation}
  L_t = \max\Big(0,\ \max_{j \le t} \sum_{i=j}^{t} w_i(j)\,\big[-\log p_s(f_i)\big]\Big), \qquad
  w_i(j) = \frac{\nu_i(j)}{\nu_{\mathrm{rel}}},
  \label{eq:absence}
\end{equation}
where $\nu_i(j)$ counts the reliable samples that image $i$ judges for the first time since image $j$, and
$\nu_{\mathrm{rel}}$ counts all reliable samples of the state. The weights let every sample testify once. Looking at the
same patch again adds nothing, and the weights of a window sum to at most one, so a few glances at the armrest cannot
stand in for the whole armchair. The statistic also needs no complete view of the surface: a state ends as soon as
$L_t$ crosses its threshold, and its interval closes midway between its last support and that decision.

\paragraph{Successors.}
An object can also reappear elsewhere, as the armchair does when it is put down in another corner. Observations of an
object away from its current state may show an unseen side of that state, a successor after a move, or a fragment
produced by a mislabel. A new surface that lies away from the current one favors a move, and a new surface that later
readings see through is a fragment. The prior probability $q_\ell$ that object $\ell$ changes, estimated from the
resolved decisions of $\ell$ and of its class, weighs the two. Once a move is more probable than not, the new
observations begin the successor state and the current state ends; until then, they remain a candidate.

\paragraph{In view, out of view, and between visits.}
The ways an object can change differ only in the evidence they leave. An object that moves in view is tracked as in
Khronos~\cite{schmid2024khronos}, its state ends when the motion starts, and the frames after it settles build its next
state. People are detected with the free-space cue of Dynablox~\cite{schmid2023dynablox} and kept out of the static
map. An object that changes while the camera looks elsewhere, or while the robot is away, is caught by the tests above
as soon as a later observation reaches its place. A revisit therefore continues the decisions of the previous visit, and
an inherited state enters the tests with the prior odds $q_\ell$ that its object has changed.

\subsection{Map Update with Valid Domains}
\label{sec:validity}

A detected change helps only if the map follows it completely and without side effects. Removing only the patch that the
camera saw through leaves the rest of the armchair in the map. The observations taken before the change also keep acting
on the map. The frames that saw the armchair would fuse it back, the free space observed before the sofa arrived would
erase the sofa, and earlier keyframes would train a Gaussian map toward the old room. A new measurement of the wall would
even reinforce a stale copy of the wall instead of replacing it. Map update removes each of these effects.

\paragraph{Ending the whole state.}
When a state ends, all of its elements end with it, including parts that no reading has seen through, such as the back
of the armchair. The armchair leaves the current map at once and remains in the maps of earlier times
(\cref{eq:map_at_t}).

\paragraph{Valid domains.}
Every observation is correct for the moment it was taken. A frame of the armchair taken before it was moved still shows
the floor and the wall correctly, but no longer the armchair, and a frame of the empty corner taken before the sofa
arrived shows free space that the sofa now fills. We therefore let each observation update only its \emph{valid
domain}, the space in which the state it measured still holds. Pixels of object $\ell$ taken outside the interval of
$\ell$'s current state, and all pixels of $\ell$ once $\ell$ has no current state, are excluded. Every other pixel keeps
its measured surface, but its free space stops a band $T$ before $\lambda_o$, the range at which its ray first meets the
surface of a current object, with $\lambda_o = \infty$ if it meets none. With $\rho_o(x)$ the range of point $x$ along
the ray and $\delta_o(x) = r_o - \rho_o(x)$ its signed distance to the measured surface, positive in front of it, the
valid domain of $o$ at time $t$ is
\begin{equation}
  \Omega_o(t) = \{\, x : \delta_o(x) > -T \,\wedge\, \big(\delta_o(x) \le T \,\vee\, \rho_o(x) < \lambda_o - T\big) \,\},
  \label{eq:domain}
\end{equation}
and it is empty for an excluded pixel. For a map optimized over keyframes, the valid domain becomes a choice in time: the
keyframe taken at $t_k$ renders and trains only $\mathcal{M}(t_k)$. In both forms, old observations complete the
surfaces that did not change, without rebuilding the armchair or erasing the sofa. Valid domains change whenever a state
interval changes, and the map at $t$ uses only the observations received up to $t$.

\paragraph{Replacing stale copies.}
\label{sec:memory}
Some geometry of the earlier map belongs to no state that could end. This is the background, such as a window sash that
has been closed since the last visit, and stale copies of surfaces that the current visit measures again, which small
differences in pose and depth leave slightly behind the new estimate. We test these elements directly. For element $e$
and an observation $o$ whose pixel sees $x_e$ within its valid domain, let
$\tau_e = \max(h_e, \sigma(\rho_o(x_e)), \tau_{\min})$, where $\sigma(\rho)$ is the depth residual of the current
reconstruction at range $\rho$ and $\tau_{\min}$ is a floor, zero unless stated. The observation supports $e$ if
$|\delta_o(x_e)| \le \tau_e$, sees through $e$ if $\delta_o(x_e) > \tau_e$, and casts no vote if its reading lies in front
of $e$, which may only be occluded. With $n_{\mathrm{sup}}$ supports and $n_{\mathrm{thr}}$ see-throughs over the current
visit, $e$ is kept with
\begin{equation}
  K(e) = \begin{cases}
    [\,n_{\mathrm{sup}} > n_{\mathrm{thr}}\,], & n_{\mathrm{sup}} \ne n_{\mathrm{thr}},\\
    0, & n_{\mathrm{sup}} = n_{\mathrm{thr}},\ C(e),\\
    1, & \text{otherwise}.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:continue}
\end{equation}
The majority is the maximum-likelihood decision when every vote points the right way with the same probability above one
half. A tie ends $e$ only under $C(e)$: a surface measured in the current visit lies in front of $e$ within the band $T$
and faces the same way. A band holds a single surface of a given orientation, so $e$ is then a stale copy, and the new
estimate replaces it rather than reinforcing it. Elements that are occluded or out of view receive no votes and stay.

\subsection{Three Maps}
\label{sec:representations}

The mechanism changes three things in a map: which observations it integrates, which elements it shows, and which
earlier elements remain. Each map keeps its own integration of measurements (\cref{tab:maps}).

\paragraph{TSDF.}
Our TSDF map builds on Khronos~\cite{schmid2024khronos} and keeps its active window, tracking, and object extraction.
Khronos' projective integrator fuses each frame within its valid domain by the weighted running
average~\cite{curless1996volumetric,newcombe2011kinectfusion},
\begin{equation}
  F^{+}(x) = \frac{W(x)\,F(x) + \omega_o(x)\,\phi_o(x)}{W(x) + \omega_o(x)},\quad
  W^{+}(x) = \min\big(W(x) + \omega_o(x),\, W_{\max}\big),\quad x \in \Omega_o(t),
  \label{eq:tsdf}
\end{equation}
with Khronos' signed distance $\phi_o$ and weight $\omega_o$, and $T$ is its truncation band. When a state interval
changes, the blocks whose valid domains changed are integrated again from the stored frames in their order. The current
map is the surface extracted from this volume together with the faces of the earlier map that \cref{eq:continue} keeps.

\paragraph{3D Gaussians.}
Our Gaussian map builds on GaME~\cite{yugay2026game}, which optimizes 3D Gaussians~\cite{kerbl2023gaussians} over
keyframes with photometric and depth losses, and FlashSplat~\cite{shen2024flashsplat} assigns the instance labels to the
Gaussians. Each Gaussian carries a lifetime, and the keyframe taken at $t_k$ renders and trains only
$\mathcal{M}(t_k)$. Within it, a keyframe of the current visit trains the Gaussians that the visit created, supports, or
covers, and an earlier keyframe trains those that the current visit has not covered. Keyframes of the current visit test
each Gaussian of the earlier map at the Gaussian's own pixel by \cref{eq:continue}. A Gaussian that GaME's opacity
pruning removes is ended instead of deleted, and the refinement after the last visit trains each Gaussian only with the
keyframes whose valid domain contains it.

\paragraph{Occupancy.}
Our occupancy map builds on wavemap~\cite{reijgwart2023wavemap}, which accumulates log-odds occupancy in a
wavelet-compressed octree with clamping~\cite{hornung2013octomap} and lowers the occupancy of the cells that a reading
passes through. Its elements are the occupied cells that contain a measured surface point. The cells of an ended state
end together, whether or not a ray reaches them again. Where the current visit first measures an object surface in a
cell that an earlier visit observed as free, the free evidence of the earlier visit is withdrawn from that cell, which
bounds the old free space as \cref{eq:domain} does, so the sofa does not have to overcome it. An earlier cell for which
$C(e)$ holds ends as a stale copy.

\subsection{Across Visits}
\label{sec:handoff}

At the end of a visit, we save the map with the lifetimes of its elements, the states of all objects with their
intervals and surfaces, and the statistics of the absence test. The next visit loads them, aligns its frames to the
common frame, and continues the same decisions from where the previous visit stopped.
