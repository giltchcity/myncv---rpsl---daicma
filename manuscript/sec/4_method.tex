\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Task, Inputs, and Stored State}
\label{sec:problem}

Deployment $k$ receives an RGB-D stream $Z^k$, camera poses, and instance masks.
An instance label identifies a physical object and remains consistent across
deployments. A rigid transform aligns the previous deployment's map with the
new deployment's coordinate frame. These poses, transforms, and labels are
inputs; the method below updates scene state rather than estimating them.

A physical object and a geometric state are different entities. The same chair
can occupy several positions over time. We represent each reconstructed state
by a \emph{fragment}: an object-specific mesh $G_f$, a bounding box, an estimated
start and end time, and provenance identifying its reconstruction observations
and deployment. Observed motion additionally carries the trajectory produced by
the tracker. Ending a fragment removes that state from the current scene; it
does not delete the state from history. The registry groups successive fragments
by physical identity and distinguishes the current state from historical states
and unresolved alternatives.

The exported scene state is written as
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{S}^{k}=(B^k,\mathcal{F}^k,\mathcal{H}^k,\Theta^k),
  \label{eq:stored_state}
\end{equation}
where $B^k$ is the current background mesh, $\mathcal{F}^k$ is the object-fragment
registry, $\mathcal{H}^k$ is the recorded scene history, and $\Theta^k$ contains
pooled sensor statistics used by the absence test. The next deployment computes
$\mathcal{S}^{k+1}=\Phi(\mathcal{S}^{k},Z^{k+1})$ under the supplied alignment.
Its output is a current scene together with earlier recorded states, not the
union of every object position ever observed.

\subsection{From Observations to State Updates}
\label{sec:overview}

Khronos~\cite{schmid2024khronos} supplies the reconstruction and tracking
backbone. Its active window is the short interval over which recent observations
are integrated and tracked. It fuses depth into a truncated signed distance field
(TSDF), reconstructs surfaces, and exports object segments as they leave this
window. Its backend maintains object fragments and the background in a dense
scene graph. Our registry receives these reconstructed segments, compares them
with stored states of the same identity, and controls their temporal updates.
We first describe the evidence used in these decisions, then the state-update
rules.

\subsection{When Has an Old Surface Been Observed Empty?}
\label{sec:absence}

\paragraph{Projecting a stored surface into new measurements.}
We sample a fragment's surface into occupied cells of size 5\,cm. A cell can be
tested when it projects inside the image, lies within sensor range, and is viewed
at an incidence angle of at most $60^\circ$. Let $z_f$ be its projected depth
and $d$ the measured depth at the same pixel. With tolerance $\tau_s=5$\,cm,
measurements have three geometric interpretations:
\begin{equation}
\begin{array}{ll}
 |d-z_f|\le\tau_s & \text{surface agreement},\\
 d-z_f>\tau_s     & \text{free-space contradiction},\\
 d-z_f<-\tau_s    & \text{occlusion}.
\end{array}
\label{eq:depth_test}
\end{equation}
Surface agreement with the object's own instance label confirms its presence.
Agreement with another label is recorded separately: it may reflect a missed
instance detection or another surface and does not establish disappearance.
A depth measurement behind the stored surface is a \emph{seen-through}
observation: the ray passed through the location where that surface was expected.
Occlusion, invalid depth, and lack of image or range coverage supply no absence
evidence. Overlap between two reconstructed meshes is not itself a new sensor
observation.

\paragraph{Rejecting unreliable stored samples.}
Depth artifacts can create surface samples that later rays pass through even
while the object remains in place. In the real-room data, the original
measurements of static objects showed this behavior for 12--55\% of sampled
cells. A newly reconstructed sample becomes reliable after its identity is
observed on the surface in at least three frames and it has never been seen
through during observations identifying the object in place. Only reliable
samples participate in the absence test. Imported surfaces are treated as
reliable by the current implementation; this policy does not provide a new
measurement of their validity in the next deployment.

\paragraph{Accumulating evidence rather than deleting on one mismatch.}
The test runs at each reconciliation update, every fifth backend update
(approximately 2\,s of sensor time). For an update with $n\ge1$ tested reliable
cells, define
\begin{equation}
 f = \frac{\text{number of tested cells seen through}}{n}.
 \label{eq:through_fraction}
\end{equation}
An update testing no reliable cell neither changes the score nor trains the
sensor model. When the object is positively identified in place, its observed
fractions $f$ provide examples of measurement inconsistency under presence.
The detector fits a Beta distribution to this history, regularized toward the
pooled statistics of objects observed by the same sensor. The pooled statistics
have the weight of three additional updates, and the fitted spread is not allowed
to be narrower than the measured spread across objects. An object without its
own history uses the pooled model.

For the alternative hypothesis of absence, the current detector uses a uniform
distribution over $f$. A one-sided log-likelihood score considers unusually high
seen-through fractions as evidence for absence. A cumulative-sum detector
accumulates this score, weighted by the fraction of reliable surface tested for
the first time in the current accumulation. This reduces repeated counting of
the same surface coverage. The fragment is declared absent when the score exceeds
$\ln 99$ and rays passing through the surface outnumber rays meeting it. We use
this as an operating threshold, not as a demonstrated 1\% false-removal
guarantee. The learned population statistics are carried to the next deployment.

\subsection{Refine the Previous State or Start a New One?}
\label{sec:states}

\paragraph{Segments reconstructed during one deployment.}
A segment arrives with an object identity, geometry, an observation interval
$[t_0,t_1]$, and the tracker's motion flag. An observed moving segment ends the
previous current fragment at $t_0$ and starts a new fragment carrying the observed
trajectory. For a stationary segment, sharing an occupied 5\,cm cell with the
current fragment permits refinement of that fragment, unless observations during
$[t_0,t_1]$ saw through every tested reliable sample of the old state and supplied
no same-identity surface hit. This exception matters when a displacement is
smaller than the object's own size: old and new reconstructions may overlap even
though the old state has ended.

A segment with no spatial overlap, or with this empty-surface evidence against
the old state, is retained as an alternative reconstruction rather than merged
immediately. At subsequent reconciliation updates, the old fragment can end
through the absence test. It can also end when the same identity is observed at
a different location and the old state has no positive surface support. This
second route uses the supplied identity: relocation can be established before
the camera revisits the entire old location. A nearby overlapping alternative
cannot end the current state on proximity alone. Refinement requires positive
support for the current state.

\paragraph{Comparing an imported state with a new reconstruction.}
\label{sec:samestate}
The new deployment initially reconstructs an imported object separately. This
keeps a potentially moved object from being fused into its old position. For
classes configured as movable, the comparison starts once the new reconstruction
has at least 30 reliable cells. This is a minimum size for comparing two states,
not the minimum number of cells for an individual absence update.
The states are treated as the same if a majority of the new surface lies within
$\tau_o=10$\,cm of the imported surface. Otherwise the imported fragment ends and
the new reconstruction becomes current. The comparison runs during updates and
at deployment completion. The current configuration exempts bed, desk, and
wardrobe from this replacement test and merges their imported and new
reconstructions at completion to retain complementary views. These are fixed
class-based assumptions, not a learned mobility model.

The state-comparison tolerance is distinct from the 5\,cm depth-test tolerance.
Its current value is also the 10\,cm surface tolerance used by the change
benchmark in \cref{sec:protocols}; the present experiments do not establish that
these two choices can be varied independently. At deployment completion, the
implementation also finalizes fragment closures whose absence is pending. This
end-of-deployment rule is distinct from simply reaching the sequential threshold;
its possible effect is included in the limits discussed in \cref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Background Update and Transfer to the Next Deployment}
\label{sec:background}

The supplied inter-deployment transform places the old background in the new
coordinate frame. Later observation rays can remove contradicted old vertices;
vertices without such evidence are retained, and the new reconstruction is added.
The background uses the existing ray-verification depth tolerance rather than
the object-specific sequential test above. This is an evidence-gated mesh update,
not joint TSDF fusion. Consequently, two slightly misaligned reconstructions of
the same wall can both survive when neither is contradicted by free space.

\label{sec:contract}
At completion, the deployment exports the current geometry and fragment registry,
including time intervals and provenance, together with the scene history and
pooled sensor statistics. A full scene snapshot is recorded at each reconciliation
update. These are discrete recorded states; the snapshot interval does not imply
additional sensor observations between updates. The archive uses lossless
delta-coded snapshots, with a reported compression factor of approximately
$30\times$, and the next independent process reads it through the same map
interface. This transfer concerns scene memory. It is not a claim of restoring
the complete live optimizer, active-window state, or every ray index, nor of
numerical equivalence to uninterrupted execution.
