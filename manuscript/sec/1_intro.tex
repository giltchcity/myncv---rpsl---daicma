\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The world does not reset when the mapper does. A robot maps an apartment, leaves, and returns. Meanwhile, someone has
moved four pieces of furniture and taken two away, and the bathroom is as it was. During the new visit, people walk past
and objects change while the camera looks elsewhere. Whether it fuses a truncated signed distance field (TSDF),
optimizes 3D Gaussians, or accumulates occupancy, the robot needs a map that removes what changed, keeps what is merely
unobserved, and keeps following the changes of the present visit. A mapper without change handling has two choices. It
can reload the old map, which leaves each moved object in two places, or start over, which discards every room the robot
leaves unobserved (\cref{fig:problem}).

Dynamic mapping follows change while a mapper runs. Object-level systems track and reconstruct moving
objects~\cite{runz2017cofusion,xu2019midfusion}, Dynablox detects moving points~\cite{schmid2023dynablox}, and Khronos
verifies object fragments against the rays it stores over a session~\cite{schmid2024khronos}. Long-term mapping carries
a map from one visit to the next. Volumetric methods compare TSDF reconstructions to find changed
voxels~\cite{fehr2017tsdfchange}, keep object submaps that become persistent, absent, or
unobserved~\cite{schmid2022panoptic}, or estimate how stationary each object is~\cite{qian2022pocd}. GaME adds and
removes Gaussians and masks the stale regions of its keyframes~\cite{yugay2026game}, and occupancy mappers lower the
occupancy of every cell that a ray traverses~\cite{hornung2013octomap,reijgwart2023wavemap}. Between visits, the geometry
persists, but the reasoning that produced it does not, and the old scene stays in the map: after two revisits of a real
room, Panoptic Multi-TSDFs still shows 57\% and 60\% of the surface of objects that have left, and GaME 42\% and 61\%
(\cref{sec:exp_main}). In both maps, the suitcase that was taken away still appears in front of the chair
(\cref{fig:qualitative}). Each rule also acts on its own primitives, so every new mapper needs a new update rule.

A visit ends because the robot stops, not because the world does. We therefore treat visits as interrupted observations
of one evolving scene. Within a visit, an object moves either in view or while the robot looks away. Between visits, it
changes while the robot itself is away. A change while the robot looks away and a change while it is away differ only in
the length of the gap and in what the robot knew before it. A returning robot should therefore inherit the scene state of
its last visit, not a snapshot of its map: the output of a visit is the prior of the next.

Two questions decide every update, and neither depends on the representation. The first asks which observations still
describe the scene. A depth image of a chair is correct when the camera takes it, but once the chair moves, the image
describes history and must stop shaping the current map. The second asks which parts of the earlier map remain. Where
the new visit sees through an old surface, the surface is gone. Where it never looks, the earlier map is the only
evidence, and the old surface stays: a missing observation is not evidence of absence. Both questions concern
observations and object states, not voxels or Gaussians. The map records where surfaces are, and the layer decides when
they hold.

We therefore separate the temporal reasoning from the mapper. Our update layer runs beside an existing mapper on the
same frames. It infers the \emph{state interval} of each object state, restricts each observation to its \emph{valid
domain} (the space where its state still holds), and decides with a \emph{continuation test} which elements of the
previous map remain. Objects that move in view, change out of view, or change between visits are decided with one
likelihood, one prior, and one decision rule, and they differ only in their evidence and in the odds they start from. The
layer ends an element by closing its \emph{lifetime} instead of deleting it, so every map can be queried at any earlier
time, and at the end of a visit it exports its state as the prior of the next. The mapper connects through an
\emph{update interface} of four operations: it reports its elements, ends the elements the layer names, accepts the
state intervals, and integrates observations within their valid domains. It keeps its own way of integrating measured
surfaces.

We connect the layer to three mappers that share nothing but the scene. A TSDF mapper built on
Khronos~\cite{schmid2024khronos} averages signed distances, the Gaussian mapper GaME~\cite{yugay2026game} trains 3D
Gaussians by gradient descent, and the occupancy mapper wavemap~\cite{reijgwart2023wavemap} accumulates log odds. They
hold the surfaces, the appearance, and the free space that a robot uses a map for, and each comes with a published update
that errs in its own direction. Panoptic Multi-TSDFs and GaME keep too much of the old scene, and wavemap's update, which
lowers the occupancy of every cell that a ray traverses, also clears surfaces that still exist. If one layer corrects all
three, the update was never a property of the representation (\cref{fig:teaser}).

With the same layer, all three maps follow the scene across visits. Objects that have left leave the map: after the
second and third visits to the real room, \prov{0.9\%} and \prov{6.9\%} of their surface remain in the TSDF, about sixty
and nine times less than under Panoptic Multi-TSDFs~\cite{schmid2022panoptic}, and \prov{8.1\%} and \prov{14.3\%} remain
in the Gaussian map, against 42.4\% and 61.1\% under GaME's update. What a visit does not see is kept: when the second visit to a simulated apartment observes only
part of it, the three maps reach a geometry F1 of \prov{99.2} (TSDF), \prov{96.7} (Gaussians), and \prov{94.9}
(occupancy) against the complete scene, compared with 92.1, 92.0, and 91.2 under Panoptic Multi-TSDFs, GaME, and wavemap
and 83.6 to 85.4 for maps rebuilt from scratch. The renders show the difference: on held-out frames of the last visit, the
Gaussian map renders \prov{3.09}, \prov{0.96}, and \prov{1.47}\,dB better than GaME's update on the simulated apartment,
the real room, and Flat.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a formulation of persistent mapping in which visits are interrupted observations of one evolving scene: changes
  in view, out of view, and between visits are decided by one likelihood, one prior, and one decision rule, and each
  visit hands its state to the next as a prior;
  \item an update layer whose valid domains admit an observation only while the state it measured holds and whose
  continuation test keeps an element of the earlier map until current observations place its surface elsewhere,
  connected to a TSDF, a 3D Gaussian, and an occupancy mapper through an update interface of four operations;
  \item an evaluation on three visits to a real room, a simulated apartment, the public Flat dataset, and the TESSE data
  released with Khronos, in which every mapper with our layer reaches a higher geometry F1 than the published update of
  its representation, and our TSDF map exceeds Khronos on Khronos' own data.
\end{itemize}
