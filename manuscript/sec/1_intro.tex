\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Robots deployed for weeks, months, or years encounter environmental change under
three distinct observation regimes. First, an object can move while it remains in
the robot's field of view. The system then observes the motion itself and can
represent the entity by a trajectory, temporal bounding boxes, timestamps, and
time-indexed geometry. Second, the robot can observe an element, look elsewhere
while the same mapping session remains active, and later re-observe the affected
region after the element has moved, disappeared, or been replaced. In this case,
the transition is hidden: the system observes only the scene before and after the
change. Third, one mapping session can terminate completely, the environment can
change while the robot is absent, and a later independent session must continue
from persistent scene memory. Importantly, the third regime does not replace the
first two: a new session must reason about changes since the previous deployment
while simultaneously tracking visible motion and reconciling additional hidden
changes occurring during its own traversal.

These regimes require different inferences and must not be collapsed into a
single notion of ``dynamic.'' Continuously observed motion supports an actual
trajectory. A hidden transition does not: if the old location is later observed
as free space and stable geometry appears elsewhere, the honest interpretation
may be an absence at the old location and a new element at the new location,
unless identity can be established reliably. Moreover, no match is not evidence
of absence. Geometry inherited from an earlier observation must remain
\emph{unobserved} when the new trajectory provides insufficient visibility, and
may be removed only when later measurements contradict it. Across independent
sessions, the same distinction must survive the termination of the online SLAM
process. This requires a scene memory that preserves more than an isolated final
reconstruction and that can become the prior of the next deployment.

A large body of dynamic SLAM work addresses the first regime. Systems such as
Co-Fusion and MaskFusion jointly segment, track, and reconstruct moving
objects~\cite{runz2017cofusion,runz2018maskfusion}, while DynaSLAM and related
approaches protect camera tracking and static mapping by rejecting dynamic
measurements~\cite{bescos2018dynaslam}. More recent systems estimate motion more
explicitly: Dynablox detects diverse moving geometry from conservative free-space
evidence~\cite{schmid2023dynablox}, and DynoSAM jointly optimizes camera state,
object motion, and object structure~\cite{morris2025dynosam}. Earlier long-term
LiDAR mapping also combined point-wise dynamic classification, velocity
estimation, and map maintenance over repeated surveys~\cite{pomerleau2014longterm}.
These methods provide important mechanisms for visible dynamics, but their map
representations and treatment of longer-term change differ substantially from a
dense object- and structure-aware history of a scene.

Several systems have moved closer to connecting visible and hidden change.
Changing-SLAM combines dynamic-keypoint filtering and object tracking with a
Bayesian object-belief map and long-term data association~\cite{soares2023changing}.
Its primary output, however, remains camera localization and an ORB-feature-based
sparse map, rather than a dense metric-semantic reconstruction of scene evolution.
Gaussian Mapping for Evolving Scenes (GaME) updates a 3D Gaussian representation
when the scene changes outside the current view and removes stale keyframes, but
is formulated around evolving novel-view-synthesis maps rather than persistent
robot-centric scene memory across process-separated deployments~\cite{yugay2026game}.

Khronos provides the closest dense formulation within a continuously running
mapping problem. It defines Spatio-Temporal Metric-Semantic SLAM (SMS) and
factorizes scene evolution into a fast process for continuously observed
short-term dynamics and a slower global process for abrupt changes occurring
during observation gaps~\cite{schmid2024khronos}. This directly covers the first
two regimes: visible motion is represented over time, whereas hidden changes are
inferred during global reconciliation. The published formulation, however,
estimates one evolving spatio-temporal map over a continuous temporal sequence. It
does not define a process-separated continuation protocol in which a completed
session exports a persistent scene state, a later session imports it as its
initial prior, and the new session again runs the complete visible- and
hidden-change machinery.

A complementary literature addresses long-term and multi-session mapping. Dynamic
Pose Graph SLAM and subsequent local-map systems update or replace outdated map
parts across revisits and multiple mapping sessions~\cite{walcott2012dynamic,lazaro2018efficient}.
Feature-persistence filters maintain Bayesian beliefs about whether landmarks
remain useful over time~\cite{rosen2016lifelong,hashemifar2020persistence}. At a
denser object-aware level, Panoptic Multi-TSDFs maintains active and inactive
volumetric submaps and distinguishes persistent, absent, and unobserved
entities~\cite{schmid2022panoptic}. POCD updates object-level volumetric maps for
changes between robot runs, while POV-SLAM jointly reasons about semi-static
objects and robot pose over repeated visits~\cite{qian2022pocd,qian2023povslam}.
ObVi-SLAM maintains a compact map of persistent objects across deployment
sessions~\cite{adkins2024obvislam}, and Living Scenes associates and reconstructs
objects from scans captured at different times~\cite{zhu2024livingscenes}.
A concurrent preprint, OASIS-Map, further studies object association and
appeared, disappeared, moved, and unknown states across revisits~\cite{oh2026oasismap}.

These approaches provide strong components for the third regime, but they do not
maintain a dense spatio-temporal metric-semantic scene representation in which
every new session also reconstructs visible dynamics and reconciles hidden
within-session changes.

This separation leaves a practical gap for long-term robotic operation. Restarting
from an empty map discards valid historical coverage. Replacing the old map with
the newest reconstruction loses regions that the new traversal did not observe.
Conversely, unconditional map union preserves stale surfaces and duplicated
objects. Pairwise change detection alone is also insufficient: after applying an
inter-session update, the new deployment remains a dynamic mapping session in
which additional visible and hidden changes can occur. A long-term robot therefore
needs a recurring process in which each session consumes persistent memory,
performs complete dynamic scene mapping, and emits an updated memory for the next
session.

We formalize this problem as \emph{Persistent Spatio-Temporal Metric-Semantic
SLAM} (P-SMS). Let $\mathcal{S}^{k}$ denote the $k$-th independent mapping
session, with observations $\mathcal{Z}^{k}$, and let $\mathcal{M}^{k-1}$ be the
persistent scene memory produced by the preceding session. P-SMS seeks the
recurring update
\begin{equation}
    \left(\mathcal{M}^{k},\,\mathcal{H}^{k}\right)
    =
    \Phi\!\left(\mathcal{M}^{k-1},\mathcal{Z}^{k}\right),
    \label{eq:psms_update}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{H}^{k}$ stores the time-indexed history of dynamics observed
within session $k$, and $\mathcal{M}^{k}$ is the updated current-scene memory
that survives process termination. Within each session, $\Phi$ must support both
continuous visible motion and abrupt hidden change. Across sessions, it must
retain previously valid but unobserved geometry, remove inherited content only
when contradicted by reliable current evidence, incorporate genuinely new
content, and preserve the information required to repeat the same process in
session $k+1$.

We instantiate P-SMS by extending dense single-session spatio-temporal mapping
with a persistent scene-memory and reconciliation layer. A completed session
exports stable scene geometry, object-level memory, change evidence, and session
provenance. A later independent session loads this state as its prior, updates it
using current surface and visibility evidence, and concurrently runs the same
within-session machinery for visible motion and hidden changes. Cross-session
change is therefore not treated as an isolated third detector. It is the
persistent update that connects complete dynamic mapping sessions into one
long-term robotic system.

To the best of our knowledge, no existing \emph{dense metric-semantic SLAM}
system jointly provides (i) time-indexed reconstruction of motion observed by the
robot, (ii) reconciliation of changes occurring outside the robot's view during a
running session, and (iii) persistent continuation of the same scene memory
across independent process-separated sessions. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We introduce P-SMS, a problem formulation that explicitly connects
    continuously observed dynamics, hidden within-session changes, and
    inter-session environmental evolution in a recurring long-term mapping
    process.

    \item We develop a persistent scene-memory architecture that transfers a
    dense current map, object memory, change evidence, and session provenance
    between deployments, while retaining complete visible- and hidden-change
    processing inside every session.

    \item We establish an evaluation protocol that separates the three regimes
    while testing them in a common multi-session episode, including current-map
    reconstruction, change-state estimation, preservation of unobserved regions,
    dynamic-to-static leakage, and cross-session update cost.

\end{itemize}
