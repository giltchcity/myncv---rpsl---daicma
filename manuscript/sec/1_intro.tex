\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A robot revisiting a room must update its map to the new furniture arrangement while retaining unchanged
geometry. The same requirement arises during a visit, when an object moves in view
or changes position while the robot looks elsewhere (\cref{fig:teaser}). Such changes occur throughout
long-term operation, so mapping must reconstruct the changing scene from successive, partial observations.
Accurate geometric maps support navigation and interaction, while renderable maps provide a visual model
for inspecting the evolving environment.

Recent mapping systems have established substantial capabilities in these settings. Khronos introduced
the first real-time system for metric-semantic reconstruction of a scene over time~\cite{schmid2024khronos}.
GaME introduced the first mapping system with novel-view synthesis for evolving, long-term dynamic
scenes~\cite{yugay2026game}. wavemap combines state-of-the-art lossless occupancy compression with
efficient multi-resolution integration~\cite{reijgwart2023wavemap}. These systems reconstruct surfaces,
Gaussians, or occupancy, but all must decide how a scene change affects the geometry already stored and
the measurements that continue to update it.

Consider a chair that has moved. Current depth may reveal the floor behind part of its old location,
providing evidence that the old state has ended. Updating the reconstruction must then account for the
chair's stored geometry, including parts outside the current view. It must also account for earlier
observations that remain in fusion or optimization: old images still depict the chair, and old free-space
measurements may pass through its new location. Yet those same observations can contain useful information
about the unchanged wall. A change therefore affects both map content and the spatial and temporal scope
of observation contributions.

We use the interval in which a scene state exists as a common basis for these two updates. Measurements
of a state contribute to its reconstruction; a confirmed change ends its geometry in the current map and
revises where earlier measurements can act. For example, an earlier depth measurement of the wall can
retain its surface contribution while its free-space update stops before a newly placed object.
This distinction lets new observations reconstruct the changed scene and earlier observations continue
to complete the parts that remain consistent.

We introduce \ours, a unified dynamic mapping method with two components, \emph{change detection} and
\emph{map update} (\cref{fig:method}). Change detection checks retained surfaces against current depth
and combines this evidence with motion cues to determine which states continue, end, or begin.
Map update applies these decisions to geometry and observation contributions. The same procedure continues
when observations resume on another visit and governs TSDF fusion, Gaussian optimization, and occupancy
integration. Geometry that is occluded or left unobserved is retained until evidence supports an update;
ended states remain available for queries at earlier times.

Our real and synthetic sequences contain changes both within and across visits in the same evolving map.
Flat and 3RScan provide further cross-visit evaluation. Across these four datasets, all three representations
improve geometry, object reconstruction, and change detection over their corresponding baselines
(\cref{tab:main}). Partial revisits demonstrate greater completeness, and TESSE provides an additional
evaluation of temporal reconstruction. We examine Gaussian reconstruction in detail, with
0.96--3.43\,dB higher novel-view PSNR than GaME across the four datasets and ablations that measure the
effects of geometry retirement and observation reuse.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A unified dynamic mapping method that uses scene-state intervals to coordinate geometry and observation
  updates within and across visits, realized in TSDF, Gaussian, and occupancy reconstruction.
  \item An evaluation of continuous reconstruction under both types of change, with public benchmarks,
  temporal and completeness evaluations, and detailed Gaussian rendering results and mechanism ablations.
\end{itemize}
