\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A robot maps an apartment, leaves, and returns twelve days later. Meanwhile, someone has moved four pieces of
furniture and taken two away, and the bathroom is as it was. Whether it fuses a truncated signed distance field (TSDF),
optimizes 3D Gaussians, or accumulates occupancy, a mapper without change handling has two choices. It can reload the old
map, which leaves each moved object in two places, or start over, which discards every room the robot leaves
unobserved. A correct update removes what changed, keeps what is merely unobserved, and records when each state held.

Existing systems build this update into their own representation. Volumetric methods compare TSDF reconstructions to
find changed voxels~\cite{fehr2017tsdfchange}, keep object submaps that become persistent, absent, or
unobserved~\cite{schmid2022panoptic}, or estimate how stationary each object is~\cite{qian2022pocd}. Khronos verifies
object fragments against the rays it stores over a session~\cite{schmid2024khronos}. GaME adds and removes Gaussians and masks
the stale regions of its keyframes~\cite{yugay2026game}. Occupancy mappers lower the occupancy of every cell that a ray
traverses~\cite{hornung2013octomap,reijgwart2023wavemap}. Each rule acts on its own primitives, so every new mapper
needs a new update rule.

Two questions decide every update, and neither depends on the representation. The first asks which observations still
describe the scene. A depth image of a chair was correct when the camera took it, but once the chair moves, the image
describes history and must stop shaping the current map. The second asks which parts of the earlier map remain. Where
the new run sees through an old surface, the surface is gone. Where the new run never looks, the earlier map is the
only evidence, and the old surface stays. Both questions concern observations and object states, not voxels or
Gaussians.

We therefore separate the temporal reasoning from the mapper. Our update layer runs beside an existing mapper on the
same frames. From these it infers the \emph{state interval} of each object state,
restricts each observation to its \emph{valid domain} (the space where its state still holds), and decides with a
\emph{continuation test} which elements of the previous map remain.
Objects move in view, change out of view while the robot maps elsewhere, and change between runs. The layer decides
all three with one likelihood, one prior, and one decision rule, and they differ only in their evidence and in the odds they start from.
The mapper connects to the layer through an \emph{update interface} of four operations: it reports its elements, ends the elements the layer names, accepts the
state intervals, and integrates observations within their valid domains. It keeps its own way of integrating measured surfaces. We
connect the layer to a TSDF mapper built on Khronos~\cite{schmid2024khronos}, to the Gaussian mapper
GaME~\cite{yugay2026game}, and to the occupancy mapper wavemap~\cite{reijgwart2023wavemap}. Because the layer ends an element by
closing its \emph{lifetime} instead of deleting it, every map can also be queried at any earlier time, across runs.

With the same layer, all three mappers remove obsolete states and keep the coverage of earlier runs. On three
recordings of a real room taken over twelve days, the three maps detect at least 15 of the 16 change events between the
first two runs and all 10 between the last two. After the revisits, \prov{0.9\%} and \prov{7.0\%} of the old object
surfaces remain in the TSDF, against 57.1\% and 60.1\% for Panoptic Multi-TSDFs~\cite{schmid2022panoptic}. In the
Gaussian map, \prov{8.1\%} and \prov{14.3\%} remain, against 42.4\% and 61.1\% under GaME's own update. Where the later runs observe, all three maps reach a higher geometry F1 than under the published
update of their representation. Where they do not, the memory of earlier runs completes the map. On a simulated apartment whose
second run observes only part of what the first run mapped, the updated maps reach a geometry F1 of \prov{98.5}
(TSDF), \prov{96.7} (Gaussians), and \prov{94.9} (occupancy) against the complete scene, compared with 92.1, 92.0,
and 91.2 under Panoptic Multi-TSDFs, GaME, and wavemap and 83.6 to 85.4 for maps rebuilt from scratch. On held-out frames of the last run, the Gaussian map renders
\prov{3.09}, \prov{0.96}, and \prov{1.47}\,dB better than GaME's update on the simulated apartment, the real room, and
Flat, and rendering each frame with the map of its own time adds 1.63 and 0.51\,dB on the simulated apartment and the real
room.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a formulation of persistent map updating with three decisions: state intervals from one likelihood, one
  prior, and one decision rule for changes in view, out of view, and between runs; valid domains that admit an
  observation only while the state it measured holds; and a continuation test that keeps an element of the earlier map
  until current observations place its surface elsewhere;
  \item an update interface of four operations through which the same temporal decisions update a TSDF, a 3D Gaussian map,
  and an occupancy map;
  \item an evaluation on a real room and a simulated apartment, in which every mapper with our layer detects at least 15
  of the 16 real change events between the first two runs and all 10 between the last two and reaches a higher geometry
  F1 on the revisits than the published update of its representation, and on the public Flat dataset, where, after the
  scene changes, the TSDF and Gaussian maps reach a higher geometry F1 than Panoptic Multi-TSDFs and GaME.
\end{itemize}
