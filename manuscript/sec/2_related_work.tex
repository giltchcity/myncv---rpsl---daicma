\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

We organize prior work according to the observation regime it models and the
scene representation it preserves. We use \emph{visible dynamics} for motion
observed continuously during a session, \emph{hidden change} for a transition
that occurs outside the current view while the same session remains active, and
\emph{inter-session change} for changes between completed independent mapping
sessions. These categories describe the evidence available to the estimator;
they are not interchangeable uses of the word ``dynamic.''

\subsection{Visible dynamics and dynamic SLAM}

A first family of methods models entities that move while they are observed.
Co-Fusion maintains separate surfel models for the background and multiple
foreground objects, jointly segmenting, tracking, and reconstructing their rigid
motion from a live RGB-D stream~\cite{runz2017cofusion}. MaskFusion adds
instance-level semantic recognition and an object-aware map, but its own
limitations restrict tracking and reconstruction to rigid objects; non-rigid
objects such as people are removed rather than reconstructed~\cite{runz2018maskfusion}.
These systems therefore provide rich time-indexed object geometry for visible
motion, but do not reason about an object that changes after it has left the
active observation stream.

Other dynamic-scene systems prioritize a reliable static map. DynaSLAM combines
semantic and multi-view geometric masks to exclude moving or movable content
from tracking and mapping, and reconstructs the background hidden by those
objects~\cite{bescos2018dynaslam}. Its output is deliberately a static reusable
map rather than a retained history of moving entities. Dynablox detects moving
points by constructing a spatio-temporally conservative estimate of free space
inside an online volumetric map~\cite{schmid2023dynablox}. It is an
appearance-agnostic motion detector, not an estimator of object pose or motion.
By contrast, DynoSAM explicitly defines dynamic SLAM as estimating object
motions or poses together with the camera trajectory and static structure, and
implements several such formulations in a joint factor-graph framework~\cite{morris2025dynosam}.
These methods establish strong solutions for continuously observed motion, but
do not maintain a scene belief through unobserved rearrangements and completed
deployment boundaries.

\subsection{Hidden changes during an active mapping process}

When the robot does not continuously observe an entity, frame-to-frame tracking
is insufficient. Bore \etal model objects in a large environment as undergoing
frequent local motion and rarer global jumps while the robot observes only a
subset of the map~\cite{bore2019movable}. Their linked sampling and Kalman-filter
processes address long-gap object identity and association, but operate as an
object tracker rather than a dense scene reconstruction system.
Changing-SLAM explicitly distinguishes objects moving in front of the robot from
objects moved or replaced after an area has already been mapped~\cite{soares2023changing}.
It combines dynamic-keypoint filtering, an object-level Bayesian belief map, and
long-term association within ORB-SLAM3. Its final geometric output nevertheless
remains an ORB-feature sparse map, and its evaluation centers on camera
trajectory robustness rather than dense time-indexed scene reconstruction.

Khronos is the closest dense formulation for the first two regimes. It
factorizes spatio-temporal metric-semantic SLAM into an active temporal window
for continuously observed motion and a global process that reconciles fragments
across longer observation gaps~\cite{schmid2024khronos}. Dynamic entities are
represented by trajectories and time-indexed geometry, while ray-based evidence
supports reasoning about abrupt changes. The published formulation estimates
one evolving spatio-temporal map along a continuous temporal sequence, however;
it does not specify or evaluate an export/import protocol through which a
completed process becomes the initial scene belief of a later independent
session.

GaME addresses out-of-view changes in an online 3D Gaussian map~\cite{yugay2026game}.
Its dynamic scene adaptation adds newly observed Gaussians and removes geometry
contradicted by color and depth, while keyframe masking prevents stale
observations from corrupting multi-view optimization. GaME receives camera poses
and panoptic segmentation from external estimators and targets rendering-quality
preservation rather than object trajectories or process-separated metric-semantic
scene memory. It is therefore a strong dense hidden-change baseline, but not a
complete visible-dynamic SLAM and multi-session memory system.

\subsection{Long-term and multi-session map maintenance}

Long-term mapping predates recent object-centric systems. Pomerleau \etal jointly
update a 3D LiDAR map, infer point-wise static/dynamic probabilities, and estimate
velocities from consecutive point clouds over surveys spanning seven
months~\cite{pomerleau2014longterm}. This work is an important counterexample to
any broad claim of first combining dynamic tracking with long-term map update.
Its representation is nevertheless point-level and non-semantic, does not
maintain object identities or dense free space, and explicitly notes ambiguity
for periodic objects. L\'azaro \etal present a full pose-graph SLAM system for
single or multiple sessions, updating 2D local point-cloud maps and pruning
outdated graph nodes after inter- and intra-session loop closures~\cite{lazaro2018efficient}.
It provides explicit multi-session geometric maintenance, but not time-indexed
dynamic-object reconstruction.

Dense object-aware methods model longer-term change more explicitly. Panoptic
Multi-TSDFs separates the scene into active and inactive volumetric submaps and
updates inactive entities as persistent, absent, or unobserved using current
surface and free-space conflicts~\cite{schmid2022panoptic}. The paper receives
poses from an external estimator and explicitly leaves short-term object tracking
for future work. POCD targets objects that change between robot runs and
maintains Gaussian--Beta object beliefs over geometric change and
stationarity~\cite{qian2022pocd}. Its assumptions include bounded rigid objects,
prior type and dimension information, and externally supplied localization.
POV-SLAM removes the latter localization assumption by jointly estimating robot
pose and semi-static object consistency in a variational factor graph, including
repeated warehouse traversals separated by months~\cite{qian2023povslam}.
Neither POCD nor POV-SLAM reconstructs the complete trajectories and temporal
geometry of objects moving during every current session.

Persistent maps can also be optimized for localization or object reconstruction.
ObVi-SLAM alternates a deployment-time visual SLAM phase with long-term object-map
extraction, and uses the previous deployment's persistent-object map as a prior
for the next session~\cite{adkins2024obvislam}. It focuses on static object
classes and long-term localization rather than dynamic scene history. Living
Scenes takes instance-segmented scans captured at irregular times and jointly
matches, registers, and reconstructs corresponding objects so that their models
improve as scans accumulate~\cite{zhu2024livingscenes}. Intermediate motion is
explicitly considered infeasible to recover over long gaps, and the method is
not an online SLAM system nor a maintainer of dense background geometry.
OASIS-Map similarly associates objects across a previous completed session and a
current session, producing static, appeared, disappeared, moved, and unknown
states~\cite{oh2026oasismap}. It builds per-session object maps using poses from a
separate multi-session LiDAR SLAM system before running cross-session
association. OASIS-Map is concurrent work and remains an under-review preprint at
the time of writing.

\subsection{Positioning of this work}

Prior work therefore invalidates a broad claim that visible dynamics and
long-term map update have never been combined: point-level long-term LiDAR
mapping~\cite{pomerleau2014longterm} and sparse Changing-SLAM~\cite{soares2023changing}
already span important parts of that space. Our target is narrower. Khronos
provides dense time-indexed visible-motion reconstruction and same-session
hidden-change reconciliation, while the multi-session methods above preserve
geometric, feature, or object memories but do not carry the complete dense
spatio-temporal mapping process into every new independent session. We study the
missing recurring interface: each session loads a persistent dense
metric-semantic scene memory, handles both visible dynamics and hidden changes,
reconciles changes since the previous deployment, and emits the prior for the
next session.
